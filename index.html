Research question

Can metaheuristic feature-selection methods find reduced subsets of the 17 (9 single-request and 8 aggregate) DNS features that maintain accurate and robust DNS tunnelling detection on Žiža et al.'s realistic ISP DNS dataset?

  1. What detection performance (precision, recall, F1-score) do standard ensemble models (Random Forest, XGBoost) achieve on Žiža et al.'s dataset using all 17 manually engineered DNS features? See the answer
  2. How do different metaheuristic feature-selection algorithms (Enhanced RLGWO, PSO, ACO) compare in terms of the size of the selected feature subset on the same dataset? See the answer
  3. How do compact feature subsets affect recorded classifier training time and batch prediction time compared with using all 17 features? See the answer

Short answer: partly

  • All three algorithms found subsets of nine features.
  • For XGBoost, the Enhanced RLGWO subset beat the full-feature baseline: F1 rose from 0.997016 to 0.997765 and errors fell from 259 to 194 (Holm-adjusted p = 0.000565).
  • For Random Forest, the full 17-feature model stayed best, and all three reduced subsets showed a statistically significant loss.
  • The best method depends on the classifier. No method won every test.

RQ (a)Baseline performance with all 17 features

Random Forest and XGBoost were trained on all 17 engineered features and tested on 6,979,874 benign and 43,416 attack records (about 160 benign records per attack record).

  • Random Forest scored higher on precision, recall and F1, and made fewer errors (17 false positives and 67 false negatives, against 116 and 143 for XGBoost).
  • Precision, recall and F1 refer to the attack class. FPR is the percentage of benign records wrongly flagged.
  • The gap shows how the two configurations performed. It does not show that one algorithm is inherently better, because ensemble type, settings and class imbalance were not tested separately.

RQ (b)Selected feature subsets

ACO, PSO and Enhanced RLGWO each selected nine of the 17 features, a 47.06% reduction.

How the subsets overlap

  • Three features appear in every subset: len, digits_ratio and size_stdev (highlighted).
  • Enhanced RLGWO shares six features with ACO and six with PSO. ACO and PSO share four.
  • The three subsets are different combinations of the same size, so size alone cannot explain the performance differences.
Read with care. The search was limited to 8–10 features, and the fitness function penalised moving away from nine. A common size of nine therefore does not show that nine is the best number.

Detection results for the reduced subsets

Full results (attack class)

  • Enhanced RLGWO had the highest attack-class F1 of the three methods for both classifiers: 0.997765 with XGBoost and 0.998328 with Random Forest.
  • With XGBoost, Enhanced RLGWO cut false positives from 116 to 74 and false negatives from 143 to 120, so it improved on both error types.
  • PSO with XGBoost had the highest precision (0.998545) and the lowest FPR of the three methods. Enhanced RLGWO had higher recall (0.997236), so its F1 reflects a different balance.
  • With Random Forest, every reduced subset scored below the baseline. Enhanced RLGWO added 10 false positives and 51 false negatives compared with the baseline.

Confusion-matrix counts

Are the differences real? McNemar tests

Each test compares two configurations on the same test records, using only the records where they disagree. Holm adjustment accounts for the twelve comparisons. A difference counts as significant when the adjusted p-value is below 0.05.

  • XGBoost: Enhanced RLGWO was significantly better than the baseline, PSO and ACO. PSO against the baseline showed no clear difference.
  • Random Forest: the baseline was significantly better than all three subsets. Among the subsets, Enhanced RLGWO beat ACO and PSO.
  • These tests compare correct and incorrect predictions, not F1 directly. They also assume independent records, which overlapping DNS windows may not satisfy, so read the p-values with care.

Robustness: three random seeds

The final classifiers were retrained with seeds 42, 43 and 44. The data splits, settings and selected subsets were held fixed.

  • Enhanced RLGWO with XGBoost had the highest mean F1 (0.99786) and beat the baseline mean (0.99713) in all three runs.
  • The ordering of PSO and the XGBoost baseline was not consistent across seeds.
  • This checks the stability of the final classifiers. The searches themselves ran once, so it does not show that the chosen subsets are stable.

RQ (c)Training and prediction time

Percentages compare each subset with the full-feature baseline for the same classifier. Green means faster, red means slower.

  • PSO gave the largest Random Forest training saving: 481.2 s to 197.2 s (−59.03%). In the three-seed means it was 500.9 s to 214.4 s (−57.2%).
  • Enhanced RLGWO gave the shortest XGBoost training time (−13.36%) and cut XGBoost inference by 6.93%.
  • All three subsets reduced XGBoost inference time, with PSO the largest at −15.74%.
  • No subset reduced Random Forest inference time. The baseline took 2.966 s, against 3.002 s for PSO, 3.123 s for Enhanced RLGWO and 3.341 s for ACO.
  • Shorter training did not always mean shorter inference.
Scope of these timings. They cover classifier fitting and batch prediction only, from a single run. Feature extraction, aggregate-window formation and search time were not measured, so they are not end-to-end deployment latency.

Method in brief

Data

  • ISP-derived DNS exfiltration dataset of Žiža et al.: 35,116,452 records.
  • Benign traffic comes from an operational ISP resolver. Attacks were generated with DNSExfiltrator and Iodine, plus modified variants.
  • Stratified 70/10/20 split into training, validation and test sets.
  • 17 features: 9 single-request and 8 aggregate.

Three phases

  1. Baselines: Random Forest and XGBoost on all 17 features.
  2. Feature search: ACO, PSO and Enhanced RLGWO each choose 8–10 features, using 500,000 training and 150,000 validation records. The test set was not used in the search.
  3. Final test: both classifiers retrained on each subset and evaluated on the test set, with McNemar tests and three seeds.

Fitness function

weighted F1 − false-positive rate − 0.002 × |number of selected features − 9|. Subsets outside 8–10 features scored zero. All three algorithms used the same function, the same candidate pool and the same population (40) and generation limit (15).
